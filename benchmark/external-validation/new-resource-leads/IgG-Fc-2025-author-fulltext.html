Structure–Activity Relationship Study of Affinity Peptides for the Fc Site of Human Immunoglobulin G

Abstract

Immunoglobulin G (IgG)-binding peptides have been widely used in medicinal chemistry, particularly in the preparation of homogeneous antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs). The dissociation constant (Kd) and kinetic parameters (kon and koff) are critical determinants of peptide performance in such applications. In this study, we conducted a structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis of the IgG-binding peptide 15-IgBP, focusing on Asp3, Tyr6, and Thr15, to identify more potent derivatives with favorable binding affinities and kinetic profiles. Peptides with appropriately tuned ionic structures exhibited rapid binding and release properties, whereas hydrophobic substitutions in solvent-exposed regions led to slower dissociation. By integrating these SAR findings, we identified the optimized affinity peptides, IAPG-2 and IAPG-3, with sub-nanomolar binding affinities (Kd = 0.753 and 0.705 nM, respectively).

Introduction

A wide variety of natural and synthetic products have been reported as affinity ligands for the Fc (fragment crystallizable) region of immunoglobulin G (IgG).1–3) Among them, Protein A and Protein G, derived from pathogenic bacteria, bind tightly to the Fc site of antibodies (Kd = 0.2–7.1 nM), facilitating bacterial evasion from the host immune system.4–7) In addition, unnatural peptide derivatives and small molecules have been extensively explored as Fc binders through phage display methods and virtual screenings of chemical libraries.8–11) In particular, several peptide derivatives of 13 to 33-residues, including Z33 (Kd = 43 nM),8) Fc-III derivatives (Kd = 2.2–185 nM),9,12,13) and peptide 110,14) (Kd = 225 nM, Fig. 1), are known to bind to the Fc region at the CH2–CH3 interface, with dissociation constants (Kd) in the nanomolar range. With the increasing use of biomedicine, multiple studies over the past decade have explored the medicinal applications of IgG-binding peptides. For example, antibody–drug conjugates (ADC) can be prepared using IgG-binding peptides tethered to cytotoxic agents14–20) (Fig. 2a). IgG-binding peptides bearing chemical- or photo-reactive groups that form covalent bonds with IgG,14–18) enable the generation of homogeneous ADCs, since conjugation occurs selectively in proximity to the peptide-binding site. Such homogeneity in ADCs frequently enhances stability and pharmacokinetics, including serum half-life,21,22) and minimizes perturbations in antigen recognition that can arise from modifications occurring near the antigen-binding domain. Alternatively, antibody-coated liposomes, known as immunoliposomes, have been developed as active targeting carriers for selective delivery of large quantities of drugs. These can be constructed by attaching IgG-binding peptides to liposome surfaces23,24) (Fig. 2b).

In addition to drug delivery, IgG-binding peptides have other applications. Because IgG has a long serum half-life of approximately 21 d,25) its binding to endogenous IgG in the bloodstream can be exploited to extend the half-life of drug molecules, similar to the strategy of albumin binding (Fig. 2c). Accordingly, conjugates of IgG-binding peptide with small drug molecules or therapeutic peptides/proteins have been investigated as an in vivo strategy to extend their half-life.26,27) Conversely, Fc-binding peptides may reduce the half-life of autologous antibodies, since they block IgG interaction with the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), which normally recycles non-specifically internalized IgG in endothelium cells28) (Fig. 2d). Similarly, anti-FcRn antibodies are being developed as drug candidates for treatment of autoimmune diseases and are already in phase III clinical trials.29) In addition, IgG-binding peptide have been utilized as trigger molecules for the selective activation of a transition metal catalyst,30) thereby enabling innovative strategies for in vivo drug synthesis.31,32)

The choice of IgG-binding peptides must be carefully considered, depending on the intended application (Fig. 2). A key index for this selection is the dissociation constant (Kd), which reflects the balance between the association rate constant (kon) and the dissociation rate constant (koff). Recently, kinetic parameters such as kon and koff have been increasingly recognized as critical determinants in medicinal chemistry.33,34) Therefore, selecting affinity peptide derivatives with suitable kinetic properties is essential for achieving optimal performance in each application.

When IgG-binding peptides are used for covalent conjugation (Fig. 2a), a higher association rate (high kon) is preferable for slower dissociation (low koff), because dissociation becomes irrelevant once the covalent bond is formed. In contrast, for non-covalent applications, such as immunoliposomes,23,24) non-covalent-type ADC,19) and half-life extension conjugates,26,27) IgG-binding peptides with low koff values are required to minimize undesired dissociation from antibodies in circulation. Thus, both kon and koff values are indispensable considerations in tailoring IgG-binding peptides for diverse antibody-based medicinal strategies (Fig. 2).

In our previous studies,35–38) structure derivatization of IgG-binding peptide 1 (17 residues, Kd = 225 nM) yielded a shortened variant, 15-IgBP (Ac-DC*AYHKGELVWC*TFH-NH2, *disulfide), which retained comparable affinity (15 residues, Kd = 267 nM)35) (Fig. 1). Further structure–activity relationship (SAR) study demonstrated that the substitutions of Lys8Leu (Kd = 8.19 nM) and His17(2-Pya) (2-pyridylalanine) (Kd = 76 nM) were preferable, and their combination produced a highly potent IgG-binding peptide (15-Lys8Leu/His17(2-Pya)-OH, Kd = 2.48 nM).36) Another study showed that the Gly9 position exclusively accommodates d-amino acids, with four effective substitutions of 15-IgBP (Gly9D-Arg, Gly9D-Ser, Gly9D-Lys, and Gly9D-Pro) exhibiting good binding affinities (Kd = 105–211 nM).38) Moreover, residues Cys4, His7, Leu11, Val12, Trp13, and Cys14 were identified as critical for IgG binding in phage display studies10) and Ala-scanning of peptide 1.35) Here, we extended this work by conducting further structure modifications of 15-IgBP, focusing on the previously unexplored residues Asp3, Tyr6, and Thr15. In this SAR study, we emphasized not only Kd but also kon and koff as new structural indices. The results revealed several notable features linking peptide sequence to binding kinetics and secondary structure, ultimately yielding, to the best of our knowledge, the most potent IgG-binding peptides reported to-date, with sub-nanomolar Kd values.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis of Peptide Derivatives

Derivatives of 15-IgBP were synthesized using standard 9-fluorenylmethyloxycarbonyl (Fmoc)-based solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) on Fmoc-SAL or Cl-Trt(2-Cl) resins. After cleavage of peptides from the resin by treatment with a trifluoroacetic acid (TFA)/triisopropylsilane (TIS)/1,3-dimethoxybenzene (DMB) cocktail, intramolecular disulfide bonds were formed by oxidation with either DMSO or methyl 3-nitro-2-pyridinesulfenate (Npys-OMe).39) The crude peptides were purified by HPLC, yielding products with >95% purity, as confirmed by analytical HPLC. These purified peptides were then used to evaluate their binding affinity toward human IgG1 Trastuzumab (see Experimental Section for details). The structures and abbreviations of the unnatural amino acids are provided in Supplementary Materials (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Derivatization of Asp3

The X-ray crystal structure of 15-IgBP derivative (PDB: 6IQH)14) suggests that Asp3 forms a hydrogen bond with the amide nitrogen of Asn434 in IgG (Supplementary Fig. S2, visualized using UCSF Chimera40) software). In the previous Ala-scanning study of peptide 1, Asp3 was not essential but contributed modestly to IgG-binding affinity.35) To further investigate this position, six peptide derivatives with conservative substitutions were synthesized (Table 1). The replacement of Asp3 with neutral residues (Asn, Abu, and Ser) resulted in reduced binding affinities (Kd = 490, 511, and 532 nM, respectively) compared with the parent 15-IgBP (Kd = 267 nM). Substitution with cysteic acid (Cya) maintained comparable affinity (Kd = 281 nM), whereas Asp3Glu and Asp3D-Asp showed weaker affinities (Kd = 539 and 762 nM, respectively) despite their anionic properties. These results indicate that the Asp3 position preferentially accommodates l-configured amino acid bearing an anionic group at the γ-position. Kinetic analysis revealed that most Asp3 derivatives exhibited slower association rates (kon = 0.440–0.660 s−1·μM−1) compared with 15-IgBP (kon = 0.741 s−1·μM−1), with the exception of Asp3Cya, which showed a slightly faster association (kon = 0.825 s−1·μM−1). Conversely, all derivatives displayed faster dissociation (koff = 0.256–0.337 s−1) than the present peptides.

The dissociation rates of anionic l-amino acids (Asp, Cya, and Glu) were relatively low (koff = 0.198–0.256 s−1) compared with those of neutral or d-amino acid substitutions (koff = 0.326–0.336 s−1). Circular dichroism (CD) spectra of all derivatives were consistent with those of 15-IgBP, indicating no significant conformational changes (Supplementary Fig. S3a). These findings suggest that the variations in affinity observed for Asp3 substitutions primarily reflect differences in side chain interactions with the IgG surface. In particular, the enhanced association of Asp3Cya implies that the additional anionic functionality may form an electrostatic attraction with the adjacent basic His433 residue in IgG.

Derivatization of Tyr6

In the crystal structure (PDB: 6IQH), the side chain of Tyr6 is oriented toward the solvent-exposed surface of IgG.14) Generally, residues in such positions favor hydrophilic side chains, as they promote hydration when exposed to water. However, Tyr6 also participates in an intramolecular CH–π interaction with Leu11, which helps constrain the peptide backbone to an appropriate conformation. The importance of Tyr6 is further underscored by Ala-scanning studies of peptide 1, in which substitution at this position completely abolished IgG-binding affinity.35) Based on these insights, we designed and synthesized a series of Tyr6 derivatives incorporating various side chains (Table 2).

Substitution with aromatic residues, including Tyr6(1-Nal) (1-naphtylalanine), Tyr6(2-Nal) (2-naphtylalanine), and Tyr6Trp improved the binding affinities (Kd = 178, 220 and 247 nM, respectively), accompanied by slower dissociation rates (koff = 0.153, 0.086 and 0.116 s−1, respectively). In contrast, substitutions involving main-chain modifications Tyr6MeTrp (N-methyl tryptophan) and Tyr6Tpi (2,3,4,9-tetrahydro-1H-pyrido[3,4-b]indole-3-carboxylic acid) resulted in very weak binding (Kd > 2000 nM). Interestingly, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) sensorgrams revealed marked differences between these two derivatives: Tyr6Tpi exhibited the slowest association and dissociation among all Tyr6 variants (kon = 0.013 s−1·μM−1, koff = 0.055 s−1), whereas Tyr6MeTrp bound and dissociated more rapidly (Fig. 3). These observations underscore the importance of considering kinetic parameters (kon and koff), in addition to the equilibrium affinity (Kd), in SAR analyses. The kinetic parameters are listed in Table 2.

Fig. 3. Sensorgrams of (a) Tyr6MeTrp and (b) Tyr6Tpi

The CD spectrum of Tyr6Trp closely resembled that of 15-IgBP, whereas those of Tyr6MeTrp and Tyr6Tpi exhibited distinct changes at approximately 230 nm (Supplementary Fig. S3b), a region previously identified as a characteristic signal of peptide derivatives with good binding affinity.35,38,41,42) In particular, Tyr6Tpi was identified as a slow-binder with a very low kon. This suggests that its conformation is suboptimal for the initial binding step but may be favorable for stable retention on the IgG surface, consistent with its slow-association and slow-dissociation behavior. In contrast, the naphthyl-substituted derivatives, which displayed enhanced binding affinities, displayed markedly different CD spectra (Supplementary Fig. S3c), which is likely attributable to the strong intrinsic absorption and circular dichroism of the naphthyl alanine residues.

Derivatization of Thr15

Based on X-ray crystal structural analysis (PDB: 6IQH),14) the hydroxy group of Thr15 formed a hydrogen bond with the side chain carbonyl group of Asn434 in IgG (Supplementary Fig. S2). Although Thr15Ser and Thr15alloThr should, in principle, retain this interaction, their affinities were significantly reduced (Table 3, Kd = 800 and 824 nM, respectively). In the case of Thr15alloThr, the β-methyl group likely faces steric crowding within the hairpin structure to maintain hydrogen bonding with Asn434, which may account for the weakened affinity. Interestingly, Thr15Abu and Thr15Val, which lack a hydroxy group, displayed better affinities (Kd = 475 and 425 nM, respectively) than Thr15Ser and Thr15alloThr, although they were still weaker than that of the parent 15-IgBP (Kd = 267 nM). These results suggest that while both β-methyl and β-hydroxy groups at this position are beneficial, the β-methyl group plays a more decisive role than the β-hydroxy group. CD spectral analysis of Thr15 variants revealed changes at approximately 200 nm (Supplementary Fig. S3d), although no clear correlation with binding ability was identified. Taken together, Thr appeared to be the optimal residue for this SAR study.

Combination of SAR Results

In our previous study, the substitution of Lys8 with Leu resulted in a markedly improved IgG-binding affinity (Lys8Leu, Kd = 8.19 nM).35) Building on this finding, we introduced additional favorable substitutions into the Lys8Leu scaffold, namely, Asp3Cya, Tyr6(1-Nal), Tyr6(2-Nal), and Tyr6Tpi (Table 4). The Lys8Leu/Asp3Cya derivative retained comparable affinity to Lys8Leu but exhibited accelerated binding and release kinetics (Kd = 8.32 nM, kon = 2.19 s−1·μM−1, koff = 0.0182 s−1). In contrast, Lys8Leu/Tyr6(1-Nal) and Lys8Leu/Tyr6(2-Nal) could not be evaluated because of poor aqueous solubility. The Lys8Leu/Tyr6Tpi peptide showed weaker binding affinity (Kd = 189 nM) than Lys8Leu, but consistent with Tyr6Tpi alone (Table 3), its dissociation was markedly slower (koff = 0.00403 s−1).

Substitution of His17 with 2-pyridylalanine (His17(2-Pya)) yielded good binding affinity in our previous study.36) Although it caused C-terminal epimerization during the coupling reaction, this issue can be mitigated by replacing the C-terminal amide with a carboxylic acid, which leads to only a slight reduction in affinity (Kd = 75.7 nM for the amide vs 97.6 nM for the carboxylic acid).36) Therefore, in this study, C-terminal carboxylic acid structure was adopted for peptides containing the His17(2-Pya) substitution. Furthermore, we found that only the d-amino acid configuration was acceptable at the Gly9 position, at which four substitutions (Gly9D-Arg, Gly9D-Ser, Gly9D-Lys, and Gly9D-Pro; Kd = 4.24–5.85 nM) enhanced the binding affinity.35) Among these, the Gly9D-Arg substitution was selected in this study to improve water-solubility, owing to its polar side chain. Thus, we designed, a new peptide derivative incorporating Lys8Leu, Gly9D-Arg, and His17(2-Pya) with a C-terminal carboxylic acid, which we designated IAPG-1 (an Improved Affinity Peptide for IgG binding). As expected, the binding affinity of IAPG-1 was exceptionally strong with a Kd of 1.66 nM, as anticipated (Table 5). Next, we introduced substitutions at Tyr6, replacing it with Tyr6(1-Nal), Tyr6(2-Nal), or Tyr6Tpi to generate IAPG-2, -3, and -4, respectively. These derivatives exhibited no solubility issues, owing to the presence of the hydrophilic d-Arg residue at position 9 and the C-terminal carboxylic acid. Notably, IAPG-2 and IAPG-3 displayed sub-nanomolar binding affinities (Kd = 0.753 and 0.705 nM, respectively), representing, to the best of our knowledge, the strongest affinity reported for small-to-medium-sized IgG-binding molecules. Furthermore, IAPG-2 and IAPG-4 exhibited extremely slow dissociation (koff = 0.00106 and 0.00111 s−1, respectively), corresponding to residence time exceeding 900 s for IgG, in stark contrast to the lead peptide 15-IgBP, which exhibited a residence time of only 5 s.

Discussion about the Kinetic Parameters

Across our SAR studies of IgG-binding peptides, a clear trend emerged: hydrophilic derivatives possessing interaction partners on the hydrophilic IgG surface tended to exhibit higher kon values. For instance, Asp3Cya (kon = 0.825 s−1·μM−1, Table 1), Gly9D-Lys (kon = 1.20 s−1·μM−1),38) Gly9D-Arg (kon = 0.882 s−1·μM−1),38) and Lys8Arg (kon = 1.60 s−1·μM−1)35) all showed accelerated association kinetics. This tendency can be explained by the fact that Coulombic interactions, inversely proportional to distance, extend further than other covalent forces, thereby facilitating rapid association.43) Thus, appropriately placed ionized groups can promote fast binding, although contributions from the secondary structure must be considered. Conversely, rapid association is often accompanied by faster dissociation. This may be explained by the ability of ionic interactions to attract polar water molecules, which destabilize the peptide–IgG complex and accelerate its dissociation. In support of this inference, hydrophobic substitutions at Tyr6 (Table 2), a residue exposed to solvent, consistently produced slower dissociation kinetics. Taken together, these findings suggest that by designing peptides with ionized moieties surrounded by hydrophobic environments, we could yield strong affinity binders with the desired fast association/slow dissociation profiles.

Conclusion

In this study, we systematically investigated the SARs of the IgG-binding peptide 15-IgBP, focusing on the previously unexplored residues Asp3, Tyr6, and Thr15. A total of 23 peptide derivatives were synthesized and evaluated for their secondary structures and binding kinetics. The substitutions at Asp3 underscored the importance of retaining an anionic l-configuration side chain, with cysteic acid (Cya) yielding the most favorable binding profile. At Tyr6, aromatic substitutions such as 1-Nal, 2-Nal, and Trp enhanced affinity mainly by stabilizing dissociation, whereas main-chain modifications (Tpi and MeTrp) severely impaired binding despite the unique kinetic signatures. For Thr15, the hydroxy group was essential for hydrogen bonding with IgG, whereas conserved substitutions (Ser and alloThr) weakened the affinity, and alkyl residues were partially compensated for through hydrophobic effects.

The structural insights obtained, together with our previous results, are summarized in Fig. 4.35–38) By integrating these results, we designed optimized next-generation peptides. Among these, IAPG-3 showed a sub-nanomolar affinity (Kd = 0.705 nM) and an exceptionally long residence time on IgG (>900 s), representing, to the best of our knowledge, the strongest binding reported to date for small-to-medium-sized IgG-binding peptides. These findings underscore the critical interplay between kon, koff, and local structural environments in governing peptide–IgG interactions and provide valuable guidance for the development of high-performance affinity peptides for antibody-based therapeutics.

General Information

Reagents and solvents were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan), Kanto Chemical Co., Inc. (Tokyo, Japan), Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, U.S.A.), Watanabe Chemical Industries (Hiroshima, Japan), Tokyo Chemical Industries (Tokyo, Japan), and Nacalai Tesque (Kyoto, Japan). Trastuzumab was purchased from Chugai Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Mass spectra were obtained using a Waters MICRO MASS LCT–Premier (ESI). The following building blocks were used for the amino acids whose functional groups required protection at the side chains: Fmoc-Asp(OtBu)-OH, Fmoc-Cys(Trt)-OH, Fmoc-Tyr(tBu)-OH, Fmoc-His(Trt)-OH, Fmoc-Lys(Boc)-OH, Fmoc-Glu(OtBu)-OH, Fmoc-Trp(Boc)-OH, Fmoc-Thr(tBu)-OH, Fmoc-Asn(Trt)-OH, Fmoc-Ser(tBu)-OH, Fmoc-d-Asp(OtBu)-OH, Fmoc-d-Arg(Pbf)-OH, Fmoc-MeTrp(Boc)-OH.

Synthesis of IgG-Binding Peptide Derivatives

IgG-binding peptides were synthesized by an Fmoc-based solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) method using an automatic peptide synthesizer (Prelude), using a Rink amide resin for the peptide with a C-terminal amide or a Cl-Trt(2-Cl) resin for the peptide with a C-terminal carboxylic acid. The Fmoc group was deprotected twice using 20% piperidine in DMF for 10 min. The peptide chains were elongated using Fmoc-amino acids (5 equivalent (equiv.)), 1-[bis(dimethylamino)methylene]-1H-1,2,3-triazolo[4,5-b]pyridinium 3-oxide hexafluorophosphate (HATU; 5 equiv.), 1-hydroxy-7-azabenzotriazole (HOAt; 5 equiv.), and N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA, 10 equiv.) for 30 min. These reactions were repeated to lengthen the peptides. The N-terminal amino group was acetylated using acetic anhydride (3 equiv.) and DIPEA (3 equiv.) in DMF for 15 min. The resin was washed with DMF, MeOH, and Et2O, followed drying in vacuo. Cleavage from resin and final deprotection was performed by treatment with TFA:triisopropylsilane (TIS) : H2O : 1,2-ethanedithiol (40 : 1 : 1 : 0.4) or TFA : TIS : 1,3-dimethoxybenzene (DMB)36) (40 : 1 : 2) for 3 h. An intramolecular disulfide bridge was constructed in solution by a treatment with 20% dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) in sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4, 100 mM) or with Npys-OMe39) (2–5 equiv.) in 30–60% CH3CN aq. The resulting crude peptides were purified by HPLC to >95% purity. The purity of the synthesized peptides was analyzed by reverse-phase chromatography (COSMOSIL 5C18 AR-II, 4.6ID × 150 mm) using a binary solvent system with a linear gradient starting from 10% MeCN in 0.1% TFA aq. to 50% MeCN in 0.1% TFA aq. at a flow rate of 0.9 mL/min with detection at UV 230 nm.

Evaluation of IgG Binding Affinity

Binding kinetics were determined on the basis of previously reported conditions using a Biacore T-200 system.35) Trastuzumab was dissolved in an acetate buffer (pH 5.5) and immobilized by premixed N-hydroxysuccimimide and 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethyl-carbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC·HCl) onto a CM5 sensor chip. The analytes (peptide derivatives) were adjusted to the desired concentration by serial dilution in a running buffer (HBS-EP; 0.01 M HEPES, 0.15 M NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, 0.005% tween 20, pH 7.4). The sensorgrams were obtained with an association time of 180 s, a dissociation time of 600 s, and a flow rate of 50 μL/min. For the IAPG series shown in Table 5, the sensor chip was washed with 10 mM Gly-HCl buffer (pH 2.0) for 5 s because the dissociation time was insufficient. To determine the binding kinetics (kon, koff and Kd), the obtained sensorgrams were analyzed using the Biacore T200 Evaluation software Ver.1.0, using a 1 : 1 binding model or steady-state analysis.

CD Spectra Analysis

The circular dichroism (CD) spectra of the peptide derivatives were measured on the basis of previously described conditions35) using a Jasco J-1500CD spectrometer (JASCO, Japan) and a quartz cell with a 0.5 cm path length. Spectra were collected between 190 and 250 nm at a scan speed of 100 nm/min, a response time of 1 s, and a bandwidth of 1 nm. Peptides were dissolved in 10 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at a concentration of 2.5 μM.
